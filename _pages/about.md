I am an undergraduate student majoring in Bioinformatics at Tongji University, with broad and sustained interests in artificial intelligence, computational biology, and generative modeling. At the intersection of these fields, I hope to explore how mathematical and computational methods can deepen our understanding of living systems. I aim to translate that understanding into scientific discoveries that can be tested and put into practice.

My research interests include virtual cell modeling, molecular generation for drug discovery, and the development of AI agents for biological research. Methodologically, I am particularly interested in diffusion models and flow matching. I am drawn to their elegant mathematical formulations and their capacity to model complex distributions, construct continuous generative processes, and explore high-dimensional spaces. Much of my current work centers on these generative approaches. Looking ahead, I hope to improve their modeling capabilities and computational efficiency, developing theoretically grounded methods that address practical problems in biology.

My interest in generative models has gradually expanded beyond generating new samples. Unlike prediction for a predefined task, generation requires models to preserve structure, satisfy constraints, and maintain consistency across a broader space of possibilities. A model capable of reconstruction, completion, modification, and design under different conditions often needs to learn more general structural regularities in the data. I therefore increasingly view generation as a way to learn and evaluate internal representations. What a model has learned can be revealed through what it can construct, what it can change, and what it preserves under unfamiliar conditions.

This perspective is particularly compelling in biology. Many scientific questions extend beyond predicting a label or numerical value from existing observations. They involve searching for unobserved states under specific constraints: a plausible molecular structure, a potential evolutionary trajectory, or a sequence with a desired function. They may also concern the states a cell could reach following an intervention. Generative models could therefore serve not only to fit existing data, but also as computational tools for exploring these spaces of possibilities. I am especially interested in integrating generation with reasoning, tool use, interventions, and experimental feedback. Such models could propose candidates, undergo validation, and continually revise their representations and hypotheses in response to new evidence. I hope to help build models that enable AI to participate more deeply in formulating, exploring, and testing scientific questions, rather than merely producing outputs.

Beyond research, I have a strong interest in history, which naturally shapes how I learn about technology. When studying a method, I trace the problems it sought to solve, the ideas it inherited, and the key changes it underwent. From Transformers to diffusion models, I want to understand not only how these methods work today, but also why research developed along these particular paths. Designs that now seem natural often take on different meanings when considered alongside the data scales, computational resources, and research questions of their time. Understanding this context helps me distinguish temporary compromises imposed by current technical constraints from ideas that may hold more enduring value. I am gradually organizing these explorations into notes and blog posts. Rather than simply collecting existing knowledge, I use writing to reorganize it. For me, articulating and sharing knowledge is itself part of learning.

Following these lines of inquiry has also deepened my interest in the mathematical principles underlying modern AI. Although I am not formally trained as a mathematics major, and theoretical challenges can be frustrating, the questions I cannot yet answer increasingly motivate me to continue learning.

I remain optimistic about the future of AI for biological research (AI for Bio). My long-term goal is to develop models that can not only represent existing biological systems, but also anticipate new possibilities and help bring them into being. I hope to witness and contribute to AI for Bio’s own “GPT moment.” Advances in measurement technologies and experimental methods are making richer, more reliable data available for biological modeling. Meanwhile, progress in visual generation, embodied intelligence, and AI agents is providing new methodological possibilities for the life sciences. I believe these developments are bringing that moment closer.
